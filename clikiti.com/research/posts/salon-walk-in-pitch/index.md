Salon owners work with their hands busy and their eyes on a client. Cold email waits in an inbox they open between appointments, if at all. But a salon or a barbershop is a business people walk into, and an agency can too, with the owner's own website on a phone and a card to leave behind.

This playbook is that visit, step by step. The numbers come from [The State of Independent Salon and Barbershop Websites 2026](/research/salons), our read of 193 independent hair salons, barbershops, colour and specialty salons and salons with spa services in the US. The one to build the visit on: **only 42% of the sites state a cancellation or no-show policy.**

::stat{ref="salons/2026-q4#policy"}

## Before you go in: five minutes on your phone

Do this outside, on the salon's own site, on a phone. Write down what you find; you will use it at the desk and on the card. Each check is one question a new client asks, with the share of sites in the study that answer it.

1. **Is there a "Book" button?** 72% of sites let a client book a chosen stylist online. If this one does, say so first: it is the thing they got right.
2. **Is there a cancellation policy near the button?** Only 42% state one. Among barbershops, 12%.
3. **Does the site say whether walk-ins are welcome?** 20% do.
4. **Can you see the salon's own work without leaving for Instagram?** 26% show a gallery or an embedded feed. Among barbershops, 10%.
5. **Does it say whether a wheelchair user can get in?** 3% do. Look at the contact page and the footer.

If the site passes three or more of these, it is a better site than most in its trade. Pick another salon, or lead with speed instead: 84% of the sites take longer than 2.5 seconds to show their main content on a phone.

::chart{ref="salons/2026-q4" highlight="walkin"}

## When to go in, and when not to

Go in when the front desk is free or the owner is between clients. Never talk business at a chair while someone is mid-cut, and never in front of a client. If the owner is busy, ask the desk for a better time, leave the card and go. The card has to work on its own.

## What to say at the desk

Thirty seconds, no slides. Say who you are, what you looked at, one thing they do well and one thing missing, then offer the card. Change the gap to whichever one your check found.

:::copy{label="At the desk"}
Hi, I build websites for a few salons and barbershops nearby. I looked at yours on my phone on the way in. Your booking button works well, and that is more than many sites manage. One thing I noticed: there is nothing near it about cancellations or no-shows. In a study of 193 independent salon websites this autumn, 58% left that out too. I wrote down what I found; can I leave it with you?
:::

If the owner asks what it costs, give a range for the fixed job below and say it is a week, not a redesign. If they say clients book through the app anyway, agree: the app is fine. The gap is what the client reads before they open it.

## The leave-behind card

One side of a card, printed, with the salon's name written on by hand. Three findings, each a share from the study and a line on what their own site does. Keep it to what you checked.

:::copy{label="The card"}
What a new client can't find on your website

Cancellation policy: not on your site. 58% of 193 independent salon websites leave it out, and 88% of barbershop sites do.

Walk-ins: your site doesn't say. 80% of salon websites don't either.

Your work: no gallery on the site, only a link to Instagram. 74% of salon websites show no work of their own.

All three are a few lines of copy and one page. Done in a week, checked again after.
:::

Leave the card even if the conversation went well. It is what the owner shows a business partner or the stylist who looks after the website.

## Answers to the three things owners say

**"Everyone finds us on Instagram."** Then put Instagram on the site. An embedded feed counts as showing work; a link out does not, because it takes the client away from the booking button. Only 26% of sites show their own work, so this sets them apart.

**"We don't want to sound strict."** A policy can be friendly. One line on the notice window and one on what happens with a late cancellation is enough, and 55% of hair salons already state one.

**"Our regulars know all this."** The site is for the client who does not know them yet. Regulars never needed the website.

## What to deliver

A fixed job of one week, priced as such:

- the cancellation and no-show policy as a short page, linked right under the booking button;
- one line on walk-ins beside the opening hours;
- a gallery page grouped by stylist or service, or an embedded feed on the home page;
- one sentence on physical access on the contact page, written from the owner's own description;
- HairSalon or BarberShop structured data with the hours and the booking link (84% of sites have none).

Rerun the five checks afterwards on the owner's phone, in front of them.

:::pitch{title="How do you pitch it?"}
1. **Start with what works.** The booking button is there on most sites. Saying so first earns the next sentence.
2. **Bring one gap, not five.** Name the gap your check found, with its share from the study, and leave the rest on the card.
3. **Sell a week of copy, not a redesign.** The integration is built. The job is the words around it, and a fixed price makes it easy to say yes.
:::
