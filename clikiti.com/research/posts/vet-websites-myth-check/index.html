Six beliefs about vet clinic websites, checked against 138 clinics

Agencies carry a few settled ideas about vet clinics: owners just phone, clinics can't post prices, every practice is full. We tested six of them against our scan of 138 independent clinic websites, one finding at a time.

The short version
  • Only 17% of 138 independent veterinary clinic websites show any price: an exam, a vaccine or a fee (Clikiti Research, October 2026).
  • The belief that clinics can't publish prices doesn't hold: 44% of mobile and house-call vets do, against 12% of general and family practices.
  • The phone is the one thing most sites get right (75%). The gaps an agency can sell are price, new patients, after-hours instructions and how anxious pets are handled.

Anyone who has built websites for vet clinics has heard a few things said so often they start to sound like facts. Pet owners just phone. Clinics can't post prices. Everyone is full anyway. Some of these are fair. Some are habits that nobody has checked.

We checked six of them against The State of Independent Vet Clinic Websites 2026, our scan of 138 independent general practices, emergency hospitals, specialty practices and house-call vets in the US. Each site was read against ten questions pet owners ask before they call, with a written rule for what counts as an answer. Three questions tie for least answered, at 17% each. The one with the clearest pattern behind it is price: only 17% of the 138 sites show any price, whether for an exam, a vaccine or a fee.

17%

of the 138 websites we scanned answer “What does a visit cost?” (a price shown: an exam, a vaccine or a fee).

Source: Clikiti Research, The State of Independent Vet Clinic Websites 2026 · n = 138 · scanned Oct 2026 · margin of error up to ±8.3 pts

The study measures what a website shows. It says nothing about the care any clinic gives, or about whether its prices are fair.

Myth 1: do pet owners just phone?#

Partly true, and the sites have taken it to heart. "How do I reach you, and when are you open?" is the best-answered question in the study: 75% of sites pair a phone number you can tap with their opening hours. General practices pass on 86% and emergency hospitals on 87%.

75%

of the 138 websites we scanned answer “How do I reach you, and when are you open?” (tappable phone plus opening hours).

Source: Clikiti Research, The State of Independent Vet Clinic Websites 2026 · n = 138 · scanned Oct 2026 · margin of error up to ±8.3 pts

The exception is the house-call vet. Only 47% of mobile and house-call sites pass. The rule needs both the phone and the hours, and for a practice that comes to the client, the hours are when it can come. That is exactly what an owner needs to know.

What the phone does not cover is everything an owner wants to settle before deciding whether to call at all. The next five beliefs are about that.

Myth 2: can clinics really publish prices?#

This is the belief the data contradicts most clearly. Overall, 17% of sites show a price. To pass, a dollar amount has to sit next to something named: an exam, a vaccine, a fee. "Affordable care" does not count.

The split by kind of practice is where it gets useful:

Kind of practiceSitesShow any price
Mobile and house-call3244%
General and family5112%
Specialty and referral258%
Emergency and urgent care307%

On the house-call sites that pass, the number is usually an exam fee, a vaccine price or a travel fee. Nothing in their work makes an exam easier to price than a general practice's. So when 88% of general practice sites show no price, it is a habit of the trade, not a rule of the profession.

A specialty or emergency hospital has a harder case: the bill depends on what walks in. Even there, an exam or consultation fee is a single number.

Myth 3: is every good clinic full, so there's nothing to say?#

Many clinics are busy. That is a reason to say so, not to stay quiet. Only 17% of sites say whether they are taking new patients, and the rule counts either answer. "We are not accepting new clients for primary care at this time" passes. So does "Now accepting new clients".

17%

of the 138 websites we scanned answer “Are you taking new patients?” (says whether it takes new patients).

Source: Clikiti Research, The State of Independent Vet Clinic Websites 2026 · n = 138 · scanned Oct 2026 · margin of error up to ±8.3 pts

General practices do best at 31%. Emergency and specialty sites barely mention it (3% and 4%), which is fair: a referral hospital takes the case in front of it. For a general practice, though, a family new to the area looks for this line first, and a clinic that is full spares its front desk the calls that end in "sorry, we're not taking anyone".

Myth 4: is after hours the emergency hospital's job?#

Emergency and urgent care sites answer "What do I do if it happens tonight?" on 83% of their sites. That much is expected.

57%

of the 138 websites we scanned answer “What do I do if it happens tonight?” (what to do after hours or in an emergency).

Source: Clikiti Research, The State of Independent Vet Clinic Websites 2026 · n = 138 · scanned Oct 2026 · margin of error up to ±8.3 pts

The question also reaches clinics that close at six. General practices pass on 57%, which leaves 43% of their sites without a line telling a client where to go when the clinic is shut. Specialty practices pass on 40% and house-call vets on 44%.

The fix is one paragraph. The best site in the study, Hidden Springs Animal Hospital in Boise, a general practice, says that if it is closed you should contact the emergency centre it refers to, and gives that hospital's phone number and address. It answers eight of the ten questions. It shows no price and no reviews.

Myth 5: does every clinic advertise Fear Free now?#

The certifications are talked about widely in the profession. On the sites, they are rare. Only 17% describe Fear Free, Cat Friendly or another low-stress way of handling animals.

17%

of the 138 websites we scanned answer “Will my anxious pet be handled gently?” (fear free or low-stress handling described).

Source: Clikiti Research, The State of Independent Vet Clinic Websites 2026 · n = 138 · scanned Oct 2026 · margin of error up to ±8.3 pts

House-call vets lead at 31%, and general practices follow at 22%. A clinic that has trained its team in low-stress handling has done the hard part already. That sentence is what the check looks for, and most sites never write it.

Myth 6: is a clean, modern site a fast one?#

The median overall website score is 77 out of 100, and most sites look tidy. Under the surface, 72% take longer than 2.5 seconds to show their main content on a phone, and 78% have text that fails the WCAG contrast minimum.

Speed is worst at general practices, where 84% are slow on a phone, and best at emergency hospitals, where 47% are. The study doesn't measure why. On a general practice site the usual first check is the size of the photo at the top of the home page.

What this means for an agency#

Read together, the six results point to the same place. The sites are reachable. They are not forthcoming. The questions that decide whether an owner calls (cost, whether the clinic will take them, what to do tonight, how a nervous animal will be treated) are answered by a minority of sites, and each one is fixable with a paragraph and a few facts from the practice.

Two notes of care. The shares across all 138 sites carry a margin of error of up to 8.3 points either way, and the groups by kind of practice are smaller (25 to 51 sites), so treat small gaps between them as a direction rather than a ranking. And a clinic that answers none of these questions may still be an excellent clinic. The study only reads the website.

How do you use this with a clinic?

  1. Lead with the price, and the house-call vets. 17% of sites show a price, and 44% of mobile vets manage it. That answers "we can't" before the owner says it.
  2. Offer the new-patient line either way. Full or open, saying so passes, and it saves the front desk time.
  3. Ask where they send clients at night. If the answer isn't on the site, that paragraph is the quickest win you can offer.
Open your email with"What will a visit cost?" is the question owners are most nervous to ask, and your site gives them no number at all.
