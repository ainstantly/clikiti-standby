Take the class first: a field-visit playbook for pitching an independent gym

Gym owners spend their day on the floor, not in their inbox. This playbook sells a gym its website the way a member finds it: check the site from the car park, book the intro through it, take the class, then talk to the owner with one page in hand. Built on four numbers from our study of 165 gym and studio websites.

The short version
  • Only 20% of 165 independent gym and fitness studio websites say where to park or whether there is childcare, and 35% state opening hours (Clikiti Research, October 2026).
  • 67% offer a free class, an intro, a drop-in or a day pass, but only 33% say how a membership ends.
  • Book the intro through the gym's own site, take the class, and pitch the gaps you met on the way in: that is a conversation a gym owner will have.

Independent gym owners coach. They open at dawn, teach the evening classes and answer email in between, if at all. A cold email about their website competes with a member's question about a sore shoulder, and it loses. What they do pay attention to is someone who walks in, takes a class and stays to talk.

This playbook uses that. It is built on The State of Independent Gym Websites 2026, our read of 165 independent gyms, CrossFit boxes, boxing and martial arts gyms and boutique studios. The finding it leans on hardest is the one you will feel in the car park: only 20% of the sites say where to park or whether there is childcare.

Why visit instead of email?#

Because the gaps on a gym website are gaps you meet as a new member, and a visit lets you meet them in front of the owner. The study found the same pattern across the four kinds of gym: the offer to try a class is usually there, the practical details around it are not.

What a newcomer needsShare of sites that answer it
A free class, intro, drop-in or day pass67%
A price64%
A class schedule48%
Opening hours or 24-hour access35%
How a membership ends33%
Where to park, or childcare20%

The top of that table gets you through the door. The bottom is what you will talk about afterwards.

Step 1: check the site from your phone, before you book#

Five minutes, on a phone, the way a would-be member does it. Note each answer and where you found it.

  1. Can I try it? Look for a free class, an intro week, a drop-in rate or a day pass, with a button.
  2. What does it cost? A monthly price, a class pack or a drop-in price, in dollars.
  3. When are the classes? A timetable or a booking calendar on the site, not only in an app.
  4. When are the doors open? Opening or staffed hours, or 24-hour access.
  5. Am I locked in? Month to month, a minimum term, a notice period or how to cancel.
  6. Where do I park? A line about parking, and whether there is childcare.

In the study, a typical site answers four of the nine member questions, so expect gaps. If the gym fails the first one, there is no intro to book: skip to the owner conversation and make the missing trial your opening.

Step 2: book the intro through their own website#

Use the gym's own booking path, not a marketplace app. Write down every place you had to guess: the button that went to a form, the schedule that lived only in an app, the confirmation email that never said where to go. You are collecting the exact steps a new member takes, which is the evidence the owner has never seen.

Step 3: take the class#

Take it seriously and pay for it if it is not free. Arrive early enough to see how the front desk handles a first-timer. Do not pitch during the class or in front of members.

Step 4: talk to the owner after class#

Wait until the floor is clear. Say who you are in one sentence, then give one finding from your own visit and one from the study.

Change the two gaps to the ones you actually met. Keep it to two. An owner who hears ten problems hears criticism; an owner who hears two hears a to-do list.

Step 5: leave one page#

Print one page with the six checks from step 1, the gym's answers marked pass or miss, and the share of gyms in the study that answer each one. Add the three fixes you would do first and what each one is: a quick fix, a content job or a build.

Fill it in from your own visit; the lines above are an example of the format, and the shares are the study's.

What do you offer?#

Start with the quick fixes and quote the content and build jobs separately:

  • Quick fix: opening hours in the footer and the contact page, marked up for search; one line on parking and childcare; contract terms on the memberships page; the intro offer named with a button on the home page.
  • Content: a memberships page with prices, a coaches page with certifications, a beginners page that says what the first class is like.
  • Build: page speed. 94% of the sites in the study take longer than 2.5 seconds to show their main content on a phone.

How do you pitch it?

  1. Be a member for an hour. Book through the site, take the class, and note every place you had to guess.
  2. Name two gaps you met yourself. Parking and how a membership ends are the most common; use the ones you found.
  3. Back each gap with one number. One in five gyms say where to park; one in three say how a membership ends.
  4. Leave one page and one price. The six checks, the three first fixes, and what the quick fixes cost.
Open your email withParents and drivers want to know two practical things before the first class, and your site answers neither.

What if they say no?#

Leave the page and go; the page does the follow-up for you. For which gap to expect at each kind of gym before you visit, read our scouting report on the four kinds of gym. For a slower, written approach to an owner who does not want a visit, see the letter to a funeral director, part 18 of this series.
