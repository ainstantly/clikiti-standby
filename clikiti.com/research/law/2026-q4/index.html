Summary of findings

Clients ask eight questions. The median site answers four.

Across 125 law firm websites, the median site answers four of the eight client questions this edition tests. 43% say where their lawyers are admitted or licensed to practise; 39% pair a tappable phone number with office hours, 24/7 availability or a response time.1

    Method in brief

    Step 1 · all 125 sites

    Read

    The home page, up to six pages it links to and one lawyer profile, read as a server delivers them: links, forms, text and markup. The client questions are answered here, by fixed published rules (Appendix A4).

    Step 2 · all 125 sites

    Measure

    Google Lighthouse (PageSpeed Insights) in a phone and a desktop render, with Chrome UX Report field data where it exists: load time, contrast and image descriptions. Repeatable.

    Step 3 · all 125 sites, score only

    Review

    An expert usability review of each home page against Nielsen's heuristics, used only in the usability part of the score. Every issue must name the heuristic, a severity and the element, or it is dropped.

    ScopeThis study examines how firms present themselves on the web, not the quality of their legal work. It is not legal advice, and it does not assess compliance with any jurisdiction's rules of professional conduct.

    What this means for agencies

    what to fix first, and how to pitch it

    For small agencies and freelancers who sell websites to small law firms and solo practitioners. We scanned 125 of their websites: 0% score under 60 out of 100, and 76% leave at least three of the questions clients ask unanswered. Here is where the winnable work is, which problems to lead a pitch with, and how to show them without days of spec design.

    5 problems to lead a pitch with

    1. 86%
      Text contrast passes 4.5:1 Quick fix

      Raise text contrast to at least 4.5:1 in the theme.

      Parts of the site are hard to read, and it is a WCAG failure.
    2. 68%
      Loads in under 2.5 s on a phone Build

      Resize and compress the hero image, defer scripts, aim for under 2.5 s on a phone.

      Your home page takes several seconds to appear on the phone of someone who needs help now.
    3. 61%
      Can I reach someone now? Quick fix

      A tappable phone number in the header, with office hours or a stated response time beside it.

      Someone who needs a lawyer tonight cannot tell from your site whether anyone will answer.
    4. 57%
      Are they licensed here? Quick fix

      State on each lawyer's page where they are admitted or licensed to practise, and since when.

      The first thing a careful client checks is whether you can practise in their state, and the site never says.
    5. 55%
      Do clients vouch for them? Quick fix

      Show the firm's client reviews on the site, with Review or AggregateRating markup.

      Your clients already praise you elsewhere. Your own site does not show it.

    How to show a prospect their problems, and a redesign

    1. 1Lead with their problem, not your services

      Open with one question the site fails, for example text contrast passes 4.5:1 (86% of sites in this report miss it), and show the exact page where the answer should be.

    2. 2Put a number on it

      Compare their site with the sector: across 125 small law firms and solo practitioners sites, the median scores 76 out of 100 and answers 4 of the 8 questions clients ask. A number travels further inside the client than an opinion.

      A rough redesign that answers those questions persuades better than a proposal, but it usually costs a designer a day or two per prospect.     A1. The research question

    When someone with a legal problem looks for a lawyer online, usually on a phone, can they find out who will handle the case, whether that lawyer is licensed, what it costs to talk and how to reach someone quickly?

    Population. Solo practitioners, small firms and boutiques in the United States (about 2 to 25 lawyers) in four practice groups: the firms a small or mid-size web agency can realistically win as clients.

    A2. Who was studied

    The sample was built from local search listings, city by city, then screened so that every site in it is one an independent agency could realistically win.

    Excluded

    A3. How the sites were read

    Pages. The home page, then for each of six topics (attorneys, about, practice areas, results, reviews, contact) the best-matching link on the home page, and one lawyer's profile page linked from the attorneys page.

    Speed and accessibility. Google PageSpeed Insights (Lighthouse) on a mid-range phone profile and on desktop, plus Chrome UX Report data from real visitors over the previous 28 days where Google publishes it for the site.

    Crawler conduct. One request at a time to each site, at least 400 ms apart, at most ten pages per site; robots.txt is read and obeyed for every page requested; a site that refuses is left out and never retried. The crawler identifies itself as Mozilla/5.0 (compatible; ClikitiResearch/1.0; +https://clikiti.com).

    A5. The website score

    Each site's overall score (0 to 100) is Clikiti's website score, rubric 2.0.0. It measures the website, not the firm, and the client questions above do not feed it. It is a weighted mean of five pillars:

    PillarWeightBased on
    Usability30%Checks of the home page for broken links and images, and an expert review of the page as rendered on a phone and on a desktop screen, against Jakob Nielsen's ten usability heuristics. Each issue must name the heuristic, a severity on the Nielsen Norman Group scale and the exact element or wording; issues without all three are discarded, and two independent passes must agree.
    Speed25%Google Lighthouse on a mid-range phone (60%) and on desktop (20%), and real-visitor Core Web Vitals from the Chrome UX Report (20%) where available.
    Accessibility25%Google Lighthouse accessibility audits (70%) and Clikiti's own WCAG checks of the served page (30%).
    Search10%Google Lighthouse SEO audits (60%) and Clikiti's own search checks of the served page (40%).
    Trust10%Google Lighthouse best-practice audits (40%) and security header checks (60%).

    Within a pillar, each issue costs points by severity (critical, high, medium, low) and by how many times it occurs; issues based on judgement count at 70% of measured ones, and duplicates of the same root cause count once. On the four firm profiles, the number out of 100 is this overall score, and each dot is one check in its group: filled when the site passes it, hollow when it does not.

    A6. Precision

    With 125 sites, a share reported for the whole sample is precise to within ±8.8 percentage points at 95% confidence (worst case, a 50% share; 1.96 × √(0.25/n)). By type: personal injury ±16.8 (n = 34); family and estate ±16.6 (n = 35); criminal defence and immigration ±17.9 (n = 30); small business and employment ±19.2 (n = 26). Differences between types smaller than these margins should be read as similar. Percentages are rounded to whole numbers; medians are the middle value of the sites in a group.

    A7. Quality control

    • Every home page in the cohort was fetched and its title checked against the listed firm before scanning
    • Detection rules were checked against the evidence they record for a sample of firms in every practice group, and revised before the final run
    • Every published figure is recomputed from the data at build time, and a statement in the text that the data does not support stops the build
    • Results are published only after automated checks for missing data, anonymity, accessibility of the report itself and the margin of error statement pass

    A8. Limitations

    • A point-in-time snapshot: sites change, and a site may have improved or declined since it was read.
    • Only public pages were read: the home page, pages it links to and one lawyer profile. Client portals, chat widgets and multi-step intake were not tested.
    • Answers are read from the page as a server delivers it. Content that only appears after scripts run in the browser (some contact forms and review widgets, for example) is not seen, which the study counts as a gap a search engine or a slow phone would also meet.
    • The study reports what a website shows. It does not assess whether any firm complies with the advertising or ethics rules of any state bar, and nothing in it is legal advice.
    • Automated accessibility tests find only part of what WCAG covers; keyboard and screen-reader use need a manual audit.
    • The sample is a structured list of real, independent firms, not a random draw. Margins of error are given as a guide to precision, not as a guarantee that the results hold for every firm in the country.
